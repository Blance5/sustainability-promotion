Sustainability issue

Well-being is infrastructure.

The issue we are promoting is not a distant environmental headline. It is the strain on student health at Georgia Tech: academic pressure, loneliness, uneven access to restorative time outside, and a culture that treats a depleted week as normal. A campus can install efficient lights and still be unsustainable if the people in it cannot stay well.

What the numbers say about us

Georgia Tech’s Fall 2022 ACHA–NCHA III executive summary is a snapshot, not a census: 318 respondents and a 4% response rate. We treat it as a warning light. More than half of respondents screened positive for loneliness. Only 37.7% met the federal “active adult” guideline for both aerobic activity and strength training. Food insecurity touched 28.8% of the sample, which matters when a trip has a fee.

A 2024 Institute report on student health disparities, using the Healthy Minds Study from 2022–23, is blunter: about two-thirds of students said they had needed emotional or mental health support in the past year, and about one-third had received services. Using the 2022 NCHA, that report also found a gender gap in diagnosed mental health disorders: 25% of cis men, 43% of cis women, and 70% of transgender and gender-nonconforming students. That last group was 23 people in the survey. The figure is not a precise campus rate. It is a reason to keep the invitation wide.

Why this belongs under sustainability

The United Nations frames Goal 3 as a promise to ensure healthy lives and promote well-being for all at all ages. Target 3.4 is specific: by 2030, reduce premature mortality from noncommunicable diseases through prevention and treatment, and promote mental health and well-being. Student burnout is not a footnote to that target. Chronic stress, inactivity, and isolation are the everyday version of the same problem.

Sustainability Next takes the same wide view. President Ángel Cabrera described it as a blueprint connecting education, research, and operations so Georgia Tech can “address and advance the full range of the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals.” Its six priorities run from operations and climate solutions to culture and the living learning campus. A living campus that students only experience between the library and their apartment is unfinished. The strategic plan’s “Cultivate Well-Being” focus says the same thing more directly: expand programs for physical, mental, and emotional health.

The Office of Sustainability’s Student Sustainable Living Guide makes the handoff to us. Under physical well-being it names ORGT trips and gear rental. Under mental well-being it points to two hours a week outdoors. That figure comes from a 2019 Scientific Reports study led by Mathew White, drawing on nearly 20,000 people in England: at least 120 minutes a week in nature was associated with good health and higher psychological well-being, and shorter exposure was not. The time could be one visit or several, often close to home. The authors call it an association, not a prescription. We agree. A single ORGT day trip is still long enough to meet that weekly threshold without asking a first-year to own a car or a tent.

Campus Recreation already treats outdoor time as a sustainability practice. Its own sustainability page says programming that gets people outside supports physical wellness, can reduce stress, and helps people connect with nature, alongside the building’s work on energy, lighting, and stormwater. Getting students into the woods is not a distraction from Sustainability Next. It is one of the ways a culture of sustainability becomes something a student can feel on a Saturday.
